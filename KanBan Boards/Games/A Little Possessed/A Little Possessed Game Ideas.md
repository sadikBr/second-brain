31. **Nocturnal furniture.** Certain enchanted pieces only move in darkness. Arrange light for ordinary furniture while keeping a dark route available for these unusual residents.
    
32. **Working shutters.** Opening and closing shutters changes where moonlight enters. Furniture can block the shutter handle, making access to the light control part of the puzzle.
    
33. **Spring-loaded floor tiles.** Compress a spring with heavy furniture, then release it to launch a lighter piece toward a clearly marked landing.
    
34. **Ropes and winches.** Attach furniture to a winch and pull it along a fixed route. Other pieces can become anchors or block the rope’s path.
    
35. **Nesting furniture.** Two small tables can combine into one heavy piece, then separate again. Sometimes you need their combined weight; sometimes you need two independent helpers.
    
36. **Portable book piles.** Books act as movable weights. Load them onto suitable furniture, transport them, and leave them on a plate.
    
37. **Haunted dust trails.** Certain furniture leaves dust that changes the floor behind it. The rug can sweep those trails away, giving it another cooperative role.
    
38. **Shy furniture.** A ghostly “Boo!” makes a nervous piece retreat one tile. Use its predictable reaction to move it from places you cannot possess it directly.
    
39. **Wake-up bells.** Ring a bell to wake nearby sleeping furniture for one command. This creates short opportunities to act beyond the lamp’s reach.
    
40. **A movable hearth.** Some goals depend on being beside a portable stove or another furnishing. You can solve the arrangement by moving the destination as well as the chair.
    
41. **Tea deliveries.** Move a trolley beside a table to transfer its tray. Delivering different objects creates a sequence of small objectives within one room.
    
42. **Shrinking furniture.** A shrinking spell lets the cupboard fit through a narrow passage, but reduces its weight. Restore its size when you need it to hold a heavy plate.
    

For movement, I would make every piece feel physically different:

43. **★ One buffered movement command.** Accept the player’s next input while the current animation finishes. Fast, deliberate play would feel much more responsive.
    
44. **Rippling rugs.** A small wave travels through the fabric during movement, followed by a flutter through the fringe.
    
45. **A carefully staged winning move.** The final piece settles, a short beat passes, and then the whole room responds with light, sound, and character reactions.
    

Sound could contribute a huge amount of personality:

57. **Surface-specific footsteps.** Wood, stone, carpet, and narrow thresholds each have a recognizable sound.
    
58. **Audible circuit connections.** A plate produces a small sound that travels toward the door it activates.
    
59. **Mirror departure and arrival sounds.** A soft intake at the entrance answers with a glassy note at the exit.
    
60. **Gentle, specific rejection sounds.** A wooden knock, a soft carpet brush, or a muted magical refusal helps explain what stopped the move.
    
61. **Subtle positional audio.** Sounds come from the side of the board where the action happens.
    
62. **Coordinated mechanism sounds.** Several doors opening together form one satisfying musical response.
    
63. **Distinct chapter ambience.** Distant clockwork in the Study, glass resonance in the hall, wind around old doors, and quiet portrait-frame creaks.
    
64. **Music that gives thinking room.** The arrangement becomes softer and sparser during long pauses, then gently returns as the player starts moving.
    
65. **A recognizable completion melody.** Each chapter gives the same little musical phrase its own instrumentation and final flourish.
    

For clarity and a more satisfying interface:

67. **Optional landing previews.** Hover a direction or hold a planning key to see exactly where the selected piece would finish.
    
68. **Whole-action previews.** Show both linked chairs, a rug’s passenger, and a mirror destination together.
    
69. **★ Visible cause and effect.** A brief pulse connects a pressed plate to its doors. Players can immediately see what their move changed.
    
70. **Blocked mirror exit indicators.** Highlight the obstruction at the far end when previewing an unsuccessful crossing.
    
71. **★ Goals remain readable under furniture.** Keep a small destination crest visible at the tile’s edge, even when the wrong piece covers the centre.
    
72. **Light previews.** Before moving a lamp, optionally show which furniture would wake and which would fall asleep.
    
73. **Passenger and weight indicators.** A small, readable symbol shows who is riding the rug and the stack’s total weight.
    
74. **Illustrated hints.** Add small diagrams to hint tiers, especially for concepts such as carrying, linked movement, and occupied doorways.
    
75. **A mechanic playground.** Offer a tiny optional room where players can experiment with a new piece before tackling its puzzles.
    
76. **Actual room sketches in the journal.** Give each entry a miniature drawing of its layout instead of repeating the same chapter illustration.
    
77. **Short descriptions on hover.** Hovering a mechanism explains its current state: “Needs one more weight,” or “Held open by the chair.”
    
78. **Doors visibly prop themselves open.** An occupied doorway should look physically held open, making its special behaviour easier to understand.
    

For character and atmosphere:

79. **A more expressive ghost.** Curious while selecting, determined while moving something heavy, and quietly delighted after a clever arrangement.
    
80. **Different completion celebrations.** The footstool wiggles, the rug waves its fringe, and the linked chairs exchange a little nod.
    
81. **Agatha reacts without always speaking.** Raised eyebrows, a knowing smile, a sip of tea, or a pleased nod can communicate plenty.
    
82. **Small story fragments between chapters.** Notes, postcards, and unfinished letters gradually explain why these rooms matter to Agatha.
    
83. **A recurring visual mystery.** A missing teacup, wandering sock, or suspicious portrait keeps appearing in unexpected places across chapters.
    
84. **Weather beyond the windows.** Rain trails, moving clouds, fog, and drifting leaves give each wing a different atmosphere.
    
85. **Life around the edges.** Moths circle decorative lamps, curtains stir, and dust catches the light outside the playable grid.
    
86. **★ A title screen that reflects restoration.** Returning to the game reveals the warmth, furnishings, and little inhabitants you have brought back.
    
87. **A playful credits room.** Let the ghost wander through a tiny furnished scene while the credits appear as labels, books, or framed notes.
    

For comfort, accessibility, and helping players enjoy difficult puzzles:

89. **★ Redo.** Let players reverse an accidental undo and compare the consequences of a move.
    
90. **Separate bookmarks for unfinished rooms.** Leaving one puzzle to try another preserves both arrangements.
    
91. **Pin an arrangement.** Save a temporary planning checkpoint, experiment, and return to it with one action.
    
92. **A small choice of rooms.** Unlock two or three puzzles together within a chapter, giving players somewhere else to think when stuck.
    
93. **Complete controller support.** Comfortable furniture cycling, predictable menu focus, clear button prompts, and optional gentle vibration.
    
94. **In-game control rebinding.** Players can change controls without opening the editor or modifying files.
    
95. **Comfort settings.** Adjustable animation speed, reduced motion, stronger contrast, and larger text.
    
96. **Separate audio controls.** Independent levels for music, ambience, movement, and interface sounds.
    
97. **Multiple save profiles.** Let another household member begin a fresh visit without affecting the original player’s cottage.
    
98. **An optional “Is this still solvable?” check.** Answer whether the current arrangement can recover, without revealing the solution.
    

And two ideas specifically for making the game immediately appealing and shareable:

99. **Finished-room postcards.** Save an attractive picture of the completed room with its name, the ghost, and a short comment from Agatha.
    
100. **★ A tiny animated opening that explains the premise.** The ghost tries unsuccessfully to drag a chair, pauses, slips inside it—and the chair takes its first delighted step. That could introduce the game’s personality and central idea in about ten seconds.
